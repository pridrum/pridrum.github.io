A graduate course on systems engineering in public programs, designed backward from its big ideas.
Students build models for public-interest decisions where the stakeholders disagree about what
counts as better. I have not taught it. I am publishing it as a course proposal, because it is the
clearest statement I can make of what I would build if a department gave me a slot.

## What it is built around

The transfer goal is that students can design a decision model for a social setting and then
evaluate what their own modeling choices did. The essential questions are the ones I want them
arguing about in week fifteen as much as in week one. What is a good model, and good for whom? What
is the difference between modeling a technical problem and modeling a social one? Is a good model
for one problem a good model for another?

The content moves through multiple objective decision making, data envelopment analysis, public
health preparedness, network interdiction and attacker-defender models, and multicriteria ranking.
Each block pairs a methods session with a case study session, and the case is where the stakeholders
appear.

## How it is assessed

The grading plan is deliberately spread across many small things rather than two large ones. A
pre-assessment quiz, an initial feedback survey that I use to adjust the course, discussion posts,
in-class discussion, participation in activities, a game, peer and instructor tutorial sessions,
at-home exercises in Jupyter notebooks, a simulation, and a three-part project with peer evaluation
at every presentation.

The pre-assessment and the feedback survey are there for the same reason as everything else on this
site. I cannot know what class to teach until I know who is in the room.

The assignment design worksheet is the piece I would point a hiring committee to. It states, for
every assignment, the purpose, the skills being practiced, what students will actually do, and the
criteria for success, in language written for students rather than for a syllabus.
